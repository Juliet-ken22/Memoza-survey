What LinkUp AI is

A fan-powered live stream for big Ugandan events — Namboole matches, Blankets & Wine, Nyege Nyege. The fans already inside the stadium are the cameras, and anyone anywhere in the world can open one link and watch through their eyes.

How it works

  1. Watch from anywhere. Open one link on your phone, TV or laptop and see the event live, from any country. You get the main view, plus views filmed by fans sitting in different parts of the ground.
  2. The fans make it. You record about fifteen seconds of your stand and send it. It plays on the big screen at the stadium, and it goes into the stream for people watching from far away. Because it comes from hundreds of phones, it keeps working even when the stadium network falls over.
  3. The safety part, for people who are there. One tap calls help if someone is hurt. A simple group list keeps the people you came with together. A lost child can be reported by name in seconds. All of it works on *384# on any phone, with no internet. Most days you will never touch this part.

What already exists today: the app, the *384# menu, the big screen at the stadium playing videos fans sent in, the organiser's own page where reports arrive, and one link you can open from anywhere to watch those videos. What we have not built yet is a full live stream of the whole match — and whether people would watch it and pay for it is exactly what this survey asks.

See a real day at the match — the long version, if you want it

Grace and her two friends arrive at Namboole. She opens the app, types in the event code CRANES2024, and makes a group called "Green Caps" with a PIN and a meeting point at Gate C. That group is just a small list of the people she came with, so one alert reaches all of them at the same time. Her uncle, on a phone with no internet, dials *384# and joins the same event and the same group using that PIN. Nobody needed data. Her brother in Nairobi opens one link and watches Grace's part of the stand on his laptop.

Late in the second half a man collapses near the terrace. Someone taps SOS → Medical; on a phone without internet it is option 3 on the *384# menu. The system reads the report and marks it Medical — high priority, so it jumps to the top of the organiser's page instead of waiting behind everything else. A short line about it also runs along the bottom of the big screen at the stadium, so stewards on the ground can see it. When a steward closes the report on that page, the screen stops showing it and the stadium's safety number updates itself.

The fun side: a live poll asks who should be man of the match. Whether you are in the stadium or in another country, you vote and have a real chance of UGX 5,000 airtime landing on your line. Grace films fifteen seconds of her stand. It appears on the big screen at the stadium and in the link her brother is watching. At half-time the organiser chooses the best clip so everybody sees it, and a steward points one phone camera at the stand — which goes live on the screen, and to fans watching from other countries.

And when a child is separated from his father, the father types his name, how old he is and where he was last seen — in the app, or on *384# if his data is finished. Within seconds it is on the organiser's page and on the big screen. The moment security finds him, one tap marks the child found, and the family gets an SMS.
